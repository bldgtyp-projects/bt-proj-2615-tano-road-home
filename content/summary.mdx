This report evaluates the energy performance, thermal comfort, resilience, and long-term durability of the proposed
Tano Road home under six design variants, ranging from a code-minimum baseline through the
**B.Public** panel packages to a full **Phius CORE 2024** configuration. The following pages document the
modeling assumptions, the results, and our recommendations.

### What we recommend

Build to the **+R-52 Walls** package: the **B.Public** R-52 wall panel (effective R-49.5), the R-59 roof panel
(effective R-58.5), an R-25 sub-slab assembly, U-0.22 windows, 1.0 ACH50 airtightness, and a balanced ERV. Do not
pursue Phius certification. The reasoning for both halves of that recommendation is below.

### What the model shows

The B.Public panel system works well for this building and climate. Moving from the code-minimum baseline to the base
**R-35 wall / R-59 roof** package cuts total site energy roughly in half, from 50.6 to 26.1 kBtu/ft²·yr. Upgrading the
walls to the **R-52 panel** takes that to **24.1 kBtu/ft²·yr** and drops annual CO<sub>2e</sub> emissions from 15.2 to
6.6 tons/year. That is an excellent building: comfortable, durable, quiet, and less than half the energy use of a
code-compliant house on the same plan.

Past that point the curve flattens hard. Adding the **R-80 roof** panel on top of the R-52 walls saves a further
0.6 kBtu/ft²·yr, the smallest return of any upgrade tested in this report. It is worth taking if the panel premium
is small, since it is a factory substitution with no detailing consequences, but it should be decided on price rather
than on energy. See [Ext. Roof](/building_envelope/#recommended-assemblies).

The full **Phius CORE 2024** variant goes further still, with site energy down to 17.5 kBtu/ft²·yr and only 4.7 tons
CO<sub>2e</sub>/year. That sits inside the 5-ton benchmark described in the
[CO2 Emissions](/energy_model/#co2-emissions) section. It gets there with R-62 effective walls, an R-41
sub-slab assembly, U-0.15 windows, 0.6 ACH50 airtightness, and a shortened, heavily insulated ventilation
duct run.

### Why we do not recommend pursuing certification

Certification is achievable on this building. We do not think it is worth what it costs here, for three reasons.

1. **The margins are very thin.** The Phius CORE 2024 variant passes heating demand by 3.1% (8.90 against
   9.19 kBtu/ft²·yr), peak heating load by 5.0%, and net source energy by 2.4%. Source-energy margin can be bought
   back with PV. The other two cannot: they are pure envelope, and there is no design move left that recovers them.
2. **One of those margins rests on an uncalculated assumption.** The heating-demand result carries thermal bridges as
   a 2% allowance rather than as calculated psi-values. On a single-storey pinwheel plan with this much slab perimeter
   and this many roof-to-wall transitions, explicit 2D modeling could plausibly consume the entire 3.1% margin. That
   work would need to happen before, not after, committing to the path.
3. **The cause is fixed by the site plan.** The 4.4 enclosure-to-floor-area ratio and the self-shaded south glass are
   consequences of the program and the massing, not of the specification. The envelope is being asked to compensate
   for geometry, which is why the Phius package needs 3" of site-applied mineral wool over already-complete panels,
   roughly double the sub-slab foam, and a considerably more expensive window.

The practical consequence is that the decision cannot be deferred. The sub-slab insulation depth and the window plane
are both early, irreversible commitments, so certification is not something this project can add back later at the
incremental cost of the difference.

### Two things that carry more weight without certification

**Windows.** All three B.Public variants are now modeled at an installed U-0.22, which is exactly the glazing
performance that Passive House comfort guidance recommends for this climate's winter design conditions. That is a
deliberate choice: it puts the recommended package on the right side of the radiant-asymmetry threshold without
reaching for the U-0.15 product the certification path requires. See
[Window Thermal Comfort](/windows/#window-thermal-comfort).

**Solar PV.** At 6.6 tons CO<sub>2e</sub>/year the recommended package sits above the 5-ton benchmark this report
uses, and only the Phius variant clears that benchmark on envelope alone. Without certification, on-site PV becomes
the mechanism that closes the carbon gap rather than an optional extra. A modest array, roughly 3 kW, offsets the
difference. We recommend carrying PV in the scope; see [Renewable Energy](/energy_model/#renewable-energy).

### Resilience

Separately from the annual energy model, the home was stress-tested against the **Phius REVIVE** seven-day
outage protocol for both a summer heat wave and a winter storm. It passes both criteria with a very wide
margin, and no envelope or glazing changes are recommended on resilience grounds. See the
[Resilience](/resilience/) page for the full results.
