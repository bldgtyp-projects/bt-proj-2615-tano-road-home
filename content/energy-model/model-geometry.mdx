For all model results shown here, the following building geometry was used. This geometry is based on the
drawings/models received on **<Var k="source_files.cad_files_received_date" />.** The model geometry used for this
assessment includes both the building and the local shading context. For details on the context shading see the [windows
site shading section](/windows/).

The home is a single-storey plan of linked wings: kitchen/living, owner's suite, office, and the casita wing behind
the garage, all arranged around courtyards and covered outdoor rooms. It is an appealing way to live on this site,
and it is also the largest single driver of the energy results in this report. More roof and wall surfaces mean more
heat loss. Larger volumes (higher ceilings) mean more ventilation heat loss. Combined, this leads to higher than 
normal heat loss which drives heating energy consumption.

The modeled interior conditioned floor area (iCFA) is <Var k="narrative.user_defined.icfa_ft2" /> ft², wrapped
by <Var k="narrative.user_defined.envelope_area_ft2" /> ft² of thermal enclosure. That works out to roughly
**<Var k="narrative.user_defined.envelope_to_icfa_ratio" /> ft² of enclosure per ft² of floor area**. A compact
two-storey house of the same floor area would typically land closer to **2.2 or 2.5**. This home has close to twice as
much surface through which to lose heat as a compact building serving the same program.

<div class="btwr-callout">
  <strong>Why this matters?</strong>
  <span>
    Heat loss increases with enclosure area, but the Passive House heating-demand target is normalized to floor
    area. A spread-out plan therefore has to reach better R-values, U-values, and airtightness than a compact one
    just to arrive at the same number. That arithmetic sits underneath the rest of this report, and it explains why
    the envelope specifications required for certification here are higher than the usual rules of thumb.
  </span>
</div>
