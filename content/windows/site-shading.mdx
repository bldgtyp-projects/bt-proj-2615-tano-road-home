The energy balance of the windows is critical on any high-performance building project. The goal for this climate should
be to take full advantage of wintertime solar gains in order to reduce overall heating energy need, while staying
cautious about the potential for overheating and increasing cooling energy need. In Santa Fe's climate the winter side
of that balance dominates: south-facing glass here receives roughly twice as much radiation in January as it does in
June.

All radiation values presented consider the local shading context. Where relevant, this context is created using
satellite images from google maps and plot lines from [OpenStreet
Map](https://www.openstreetmap.org/#map=5/38.01/-95.84) and
[CadMapper](https://cadmapper.com/pro/login/?next=/pro/home).

### Off-site shading: none

As per email communication with the team, the site is modeled as open and flat with no meaningful off-site shading
elements:

*"We are building at about the high point and will not have any trees within 5-10 ft. of the house.
All the trees are piñon or juniper and no existing tree is taller than about 10-15 ft right
now so I think it's safe to assume full solar exposure until we plant shade trees."*

This is close to a best case. The solar resource reaching the site is unobstructed.

### On-site shading: the building shades itself

Three fixed conditions in the design account for nearly all of the shading in the model, and the site itself plays
almost no part:

1. The garage wing sits between the sun and a significant portion of the home's south exposure, blocking low
   winter sun to the glazing behind it.
2. The pinwheel plan means several wings face each other across courtyards. Glass looking into those courtyards is
   shaded by the opposite wing for much of the winter day, when the sun is low.
3. The covered outdoor rooms and deep overhangs, including the porches, portals, and the roof extensions over the east
   and west elevations, shade the glass beneath them year-round.

Items 2 and 3 are genuinely useful in summer and contribute to the strong overheating and resilience results elsewhere
in this report. In winter they work against the building. All three are treated here as fixed design context. They are
part of what makes the house work as a place to live, and this report does not propose changing them.

Their consequence is quantified in the [Winter Radiation](#winter-radiation) section, and it is the single largest
reason the home falls short of the Phius CORE 2024 heating-demand limit despite a very good envelope.
