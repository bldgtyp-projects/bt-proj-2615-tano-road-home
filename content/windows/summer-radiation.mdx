Because of its orientation and its four-sided glazing distribution, the building sees meaningful solar radiation on
every facade during the summer months. The summer risk concentrates on the **east and west** elevations rather than
the south. In June a vertical east or west surface in Santa Fe receives roughly 12 kWh/ft² to 13.5 kWh/ft² per month, while
the south surface receives only about 7.3 kWh/ft². The sun climbs high enough at midday that south glass is largely
self-shaded by its own head detail, but low morning and afternoon sun strikes the east and west glass directly.

The results here are reassuring:

- The deep overhangs on the east and west, together with the dedicated horizontal overhangs and covered portals, cut the
  overheating risk substantially. The same self-shading that hurts the winter balance helps here.
- Modeled cooling demand is already very low, with no latent load at all. Every variant except the
  code-minimum baseline also passes the peak cooling load criterion comfortably.
- The [Resilience](/resilience/) simulation confirms this from a different direction. Through a seven-day summer
  outage with no mechanical cooling at all, the peak Heat Index in any zone reaches only 80.3 °F.

Some exposed and unshaded windows would still benefit from additional shading measures. The home does **not** appear to be
at significant risk of overheating, but we still recommend prioritizing shading on all windows and glazed doors where
it is feasible:

- Use sturdy, user-operable interior blinds with high opacity and solar reflectance, particularly on the east and west
  elevations.
- Where exterior shading is possible, it works roughly three times as well as interior shading for the same
  visual result, since it stops the radiation before it enters the building.
- Provide operable windows and a night-flush strategy. Santa Fe's large day-to-night swing means the most
  effective cooling measure available here costs nothing to run.
