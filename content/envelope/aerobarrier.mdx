The dedicated air barrier layers described above should provide excellent, durable airtightness on their own, and that
is the goal. A designed, inspectable air barrier is always the primary strategy. Panelized construction
like the **B.Public** system helps considerably here, since the field of each wall and roof plane arrives already
sealed and the remaining work concentrates at the joints.

This plan has a lot of joints. The pinwheel arrangement of wings produces many more corners, offsets,
roof-to-wall transitions, and panel-to-panel seams per square foot of floor area than a simple rectangular building
would, and each one is a place where the air barrier depends on field workmanship. Where air-sealing results are
uncertain, we recommend having a backup plan in place.

[AeroBarrier](https://aeroseal.com/industries/residential-new-construction/) provides whole-building air sealing using
an aerosolized polymer that "finds" leaks and seals them. We recommend consulting early in the build with an AeroBarrier
installer so that they can be included in the project schedule should their services be required. The service costs
less and disrupts less when scheduled in advance at the right point in the sequence, after the air barrier is complete
and before finishes go on, than when it is called in to rescue a failed blower-door test.
